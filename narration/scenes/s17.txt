## s17 | Label-free concept bottleneck models | 0.4 | 0.8
[[Same rung, a different {c:concept predictor}]] We are in group B of rung two: learn the concepts from CLIP. Previously, PCBM and LaBo fed CLIP similarity scores directly to a class head. Now the Label-Free Concept Bottleneck Model, or LF-CBM, trains a concept layer to imitate those scores. At test time, the image encoder and this learned layer produce the concept values without calling the CLIP teacher. The class head still learns from class labels. Label-free means no human concept labels on the task images, not no supervision. || 0.8
[[The intuition: a {x:student} learns to copy a {x:teacher}'s scores]] Here is the intuition. CLIP is the teacher: it has learned from images paired with captions, so it can score how well any phrase matches any image. The student is a frozen ResNet with one linear layer on top, and it learns to copy the teacher's scores. A language model writes the exam questions: the phrases to score. || 0.8
[[Five steps, from phrases to a sparse {f:final layer}]] The method has five steps. The language model proposes phrases, CLIP filters and scores them, the student learns to copy the scores, and a sparse final layer, trained with the class labels, turns them into a class. || 0.8

[[Follow the {c:stripes} entry onto a comparable scale]] Take the first entry of the same zebra row: CLIP's stripes score, 0.274. To standardise it, subtract the training-photo average and divide by the spread, the standard deviation. That gives 1.41. The values need not be identical; learning aims to match how this concept rises and falls across photos. || 0.8
[[Copying means each {c:activation} rises and falls with its {x:CLIP score}]] What does copying mean? Take one concept, black and white stripes. Across many images, the student's activation should be high where CLIP's score is high, and low where it is low. Only this pattern across images matters, not the exact values. || 0.8
[[Cubing stretches the images where {x:CLIP} scores highest]] Before the comparison, every standardised value is cubed: multiplied by itself three times. Cubing stretches the extremes: a score two standard deviations above average becomes eight, while half a standard deviation shrinks to one eighth. So the images that CLIP scores highest count the most. || 0.8
[[A cosine asks whether two lists rise and fall together]] To compare two lists of numbers, we use the cosine: multiply them entry by entry, add up, and divide by the two lengths. It is 1 when the lists rise and fall together, even if one is twice the other, near 0 when they are unrelated, and negative when they move in opposite directions. || 0.8
[[The match for one {c:concept}: a cosine between cubed scores]] Now the match for concept j, such as stripes. Let q bar be the student's standardised activations across the images, and P bar for CLIP's standardised scores. The match is the cosine between their cubes. W c is trained to make this match large for every concept. It needs no labels, so every unlabelled photo can help. || 1.0
[[Step 5: a sparse {f:final layer} keeps 6 of 102 weights]] Step five: a final layer from concepts to classes, trained with the class labels. Horse reads muscular equine build and riding bridle, and safari vehicles counts against it. || 1.0

[[Why sparse: a person can read 6 {f:weights}, not 102]] Why insist on sparsity? A dense layer would use all 102 weights, and nobody can check 102 numbers behind every decision. Six weights can be read in a few seconds, and each one is a claim a person can check, such as: black and white stripes count for zebra. || 0.8

[[What changed compared with the plain CBM]] Compared with the plain concept bottleneck, a language model names the concepts instead of a person, and the concept layer learns to copy CLIP instead of learning from concept labels. The head is still trained with class labels. So label-free does not mean unsupervised: the language model, CLIP and the class labels all supervise it. || 0.8
