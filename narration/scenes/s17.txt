## s17 | Label-free concept bottleneck models | 0.4 | 0.8
[[Concept labels are expensive: can a bottleneck do without them?]] The third variant is the label-free concept bottleneck model, or LF-CBM. A supervised bottleneck needs a person to label every concept on every photo, 112 of them for each bird in CUB. Label-free models need no human concept labels. || 0.8
[[The intuition: a {x:student} learns to copy a {x:teacher}'s scores]] Here is the intuition. CLIP is the teacher: it has learned from images paired with captions, so it can score how well any phrase matches any image. The student is a frozen ResNet with one linear layer on top, and it learns to copy the teacher's scores. A language model writes the exam questions: the phrases to score. || 0.8
[[Five steps, from phrases to a sparse {f:final layer}]] The method has five steps. The language model proposes phrases, CLIP filters and scores them, the student learns to copy the scores, and a sparse final layer, trained with the class labels, turns them into a class. || 0.8
[[Step 1: ask a language model about each {y:class}]] Step one: ask a language model, for each class, for its most important features, the things commonly seen around it, and its superclasses, the broader groups it belongs to. Here is the real reply for zebra to the first question, as JSON. || 0.8
[[Three questions for three classes give 59 candidate {c:concepts}]] Three questions for each of three classes give 59 candidate concepts. || 0.8
[[Step 2: drop class names, duplicates and phrases CLIP barely sees]] Step two: filter. Phrases that contain a class name go, such as zebras or horse-like body. Equine goes as a near-duplicate of equine animal. Leather saddle and ossicones on head go because even the photos that match them best score low with CLIP. 34 concepts remain. || 0.8
[[Step 3: the {x:teacher}, CLIP, scores every image against every {c:concept}]] Step three: CLIP scores every image against every remaining concept. A higher score means the phrase fits the image better. These scores are the teacher's answers: targets for the student, not labels from a person. || 0.8
[[Step 4: the {x:student} maps {x:features} to {c:concept activations}]] {tags: [calm]} Step four builds the student. A different frozen network, here a ResNet-50 rather than CLIP, turns each image into 2048 features, written e of x. A linear layer, W c, maps them to 34 concept activations. This layer is often called the projection, and it is the only part that learns. || 0.8
[[Standardising puts the {x:teacher} and the {c:student} on one scale]] The teacher's scores and the student's activations come on different scales, so both are standardised: subtract the average over the photos, then divide by the spread, the standard deviation. For this zebra, CLIP's stripes score sits 1.41 standard deviations above its average, and the student's stripes activation sits 1.36 above. || 0.8
[[Copying means each {c:activation} rises and falls with its {x:CLIP score}]] What does copying mean? Take one concept, black and white stripes. Across many images, the student's activation should be high where CLIP's score is high, and low where it is low. Only this pattern across images matters, not the exact values. || 0.8
[[Cubing stretches the images where {x:CLIP} scores highest]] Before the comparison, every standardised value is cubed: multiplied by itself three times. Cubing stretches the extremes: a score two standard deviations above average becomes eight, while half a standard deviation shrinks to one eighth. So the images that CLIP scores highest count the most. || 0.8
[[A cosine asks whether two lists rise and fall together]] To compare two lists of numbers, we use the cosine: multiply them entry by entry, add up, and divide by the two lengths. It is 1 when the lists rise and fall together, even if one is twice the other, near 0 when they are unrelated, and negative when they move in opposite directions. || 0.8
[[The match for one {c:concept}: a cosine between cubed scores]] Now the match for concept j, such as stripes. Let q bar be the student's standardised activations across the images, and P bar for CLIP's standardised scores. The match is the cosine between their cubes. W c is trained to make this match large for every concept. It needs no labels, so every unlabelled photo can help. || 1.0
[[Step 5: a sparse {f:final layer} keeps 6 of 102 weights]] Step five: a final layer from concepts to classes, trained with the class labels. An L1 penalty on the weights pushes most of them to exactly zero, which is what sparse means. 6 of 102 weights survive. Zebra reads black and white stripes, and giraffe reads extremely long neck. Horse reads muscular equine build and riding bridle, and safari vehicles counts against it. || 1.0
[[One prediction: the {f:weights} turn {c:activations} into a {y:class} score]] Here is one prediction. For this zebra, the zebra score is 1.46 times the stripes activation, 1.36, plus a little from wildebeest herds and a small constant: 2.02 in all. Horse gets minus 0.4 and giraffe about 0. As shares, zebra gets 0.82. || 0.8
[[Why sparse: a person can read 6 {f:weights}, not 102]] Why insist on sparsity? A dense layer would use all 102 weights, and nobody can check 102 numbers behind every decision. Six weights can be read in a few seconds, and each one is a claim a person can check, such as: black and white stripes count for zebra. || 0.8
[[Reading the weights shows a risk: three {c:concepts} describe the setting]] Reading them also shows a risk. Riding bridle, safari vehicles and wildebeest herds describe the setting, not the animal, so the head uses context as evidence. A horse photographed beside a safari vehicle would lose evidence for horse. || 1.0
[[What changed compared with the plain CBM]] Compared with the plain concept bottleneck, a language model names the concepts instead of a person, and the concept layer learns to copy CLIP instead of learning from concept labels. The head is still trained with class labels. So label-free does not mean unsupervised: the language model, CLIP and the class labels all supervise it. || 0.8
