## s11 | How should we evaluate a CBM? | 0.4 | 0.8
[[Four checks, beyond the final {y:answer}]] Keep four questions in mind. Task accuracy: is the final answer right on held-out images? Concept quality: are the named values correct, and based on the intended image evidence? Faithfulness: does the prediction actually use those concepts? Intervention utility: do corrections improve decisions, and at what cost in human time and mistakes? || 0.8
[[One {e:correction}, the same trained {f:head}]] Here is one measured example from a synthetic-shape experiment. Colour, shape and size determine the class. This image is a small red square. Before correction, large scores 0.78, and the head predicts large red square. Set only large to 0. The same trained head now predicts small red square. No retraining is needed. || 0.8
[[{e:Corrections} help only if the head reads the {c:concepts}]] Compare two models on the same test images. The multi-task model predicts concepts on a separate branch. Its class head never reads them, so a concept correction leaves accuracy at 82.0 percent. In the strict CBM, the head reads only concepts. One correction per image raises accuracy from 81.6 to 97.3 percent. Here, an oracle uses the true labels to correct the largest concept error first. These results use one training seed and 512 synthetic test images, with perfect corrections. They do not measure human effort or mistakes. Similar accuracy can hide very different intervention utility. || 1.0
