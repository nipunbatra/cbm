## s15 | Concept embedding models | 0.4 | 0.8
[[One number per {c:concept} is a narrow channel]] First, Concept Embedding Models. One number per concept is a narrow channel, and when the vocabulary is incomplete, accuracy suffers, as we saw in Part 8. || 0.8
[[What does a prototype mean here?]] A prototype usually means a representative example. Here the clearer term is embedding: a short list of learned numbers. CEM computes a present vector and an absent vector for the current image. Both exist even when stripes are clearly visible. They are not stored example photos or fixed class averages. The coordinates are learned features, not individually labelled properties. These numbers are illustrative. || 0.8
[[Learned layers turn the {x:image} into the two {c:vectors}]] Where do the vectors come from? An image encoder first extracts features. For stripes, two learned layers turn those features into the present and absent vectors. Every concept has its own pair of layers. Training learns the weights of these layers. With those weights fixed, a different image can still produce different vectors. Nobody supplies target values for the vector coordinates. || 0.8
[[Each {c:concept} gets two embeddings: active and inactive]] Now the mechanics. An embedding is a short list of numbers that describes something; here each one has two numbers. From the image, the model computes two embeddings for every concept: one describing it as active, one as inactive. || 0.6
[[A learned scorer predicts the probability {c:p}]] A learned scoring layer reads both vectors and predicts the probability, p, that the concept is present. Suppose that, as in Part 4, it misses the stripes on this photo: p is only 0.1. The concept labels will teach this probability during training. || 0.8
[[The {c:concept embedding} mixes the two, weighted by {c:p}]] The concept's embedding mixes the two: p times the active embedding, plus one minus p times the inactive one. || 0.8
[[With {c:p = 0.1}, the mixed vector sits close to inactive]] With p equal to 0.1, take the first entry: 0.1 times 0.82, plus 0.9 times 0.22, gives 0.28. The second entry works the same way, and the mixed vector sits close to inactive. || 0.8
[[At test time, the {f:head} reads the mixed vectors]] At test time, only the new image is needed. The trained model computes both vectors and a probability for every concept, then mixes them. The head reads all three mixed vectors, six numbers in all. Our illustrative linear head produces class scores, and softmax gives the displayed probabilities. Here it favours horse. All weights stay fixed, and no true concept or class label is used for this ordinary prediction. || 0.8
[[An optional {e:correction} changes {e:p}, with weights fixed]] To intervene, a person sets p, not the vector coordinates. Setting p to 1 swaps in the active embedding already computed for this image. The same head now makes zebra lead in this example. The two candidate vectors and all network weights stay fixed; there is no retraining. || 0.8
[[Training uses {c:concept labels} and {y:class labels}]] During training, each image comes with concept labels and a class label. For this zebra, stripes is labelled present and the class is zebra. A concept loss compares the predicted probability with the concept label. A task loss compares the class prediction with the class label. Backpropagation from these losses updates the encoder, the vector-generating layers, the probability scorer and the task head together. We supervise the probability and the final answer, rather than supplying the vector coordinates. || 0.8
[[The old problem: the {f:head} receives identical values]] Recall the blue circle and blue square from Part 8. The target is class A for the circle and class B for the square. Our vocabulary asks only whether an object is blue and whether it is large. Both objects are blue and small, so both receive exactly the same values: blue 1, large 0. The head sees identical inputs and must make the same prediction. The missing distinction is shape. || 0.8
[[Same {c:blue probability}, different {c:blue embeddings}]] A CEM still sees the full image, including its outline. The same learned network computes the candidate vectors and mixes them for each image. Here we show only the resulting blue embedding; the large embedding is identical for both objects. Suppose both blue probabilities are 0.90. The circle can still have vector 0.72, 0.68, while the square has 0.90, 0.30. These are illustrative values, not trained outputs. The same probability does not force the same vector. || 0.8
[[Why would training keep the {x:shape} information?]] The concept labels ask the network to recognise that both objects are blue and small. They do not require identical embedding coordinates. The class labels ask for different answers: A for the circle and B for the square. Through the task loss, those labels can encourage the embeddings to retain useful shape information. We still have no shape concept label. This is a possible solution, not a guarantee of what training will learn. || 0.8
[[Different vectors let the same {f:head} give different answers]] Now apply a tiny illustrative head. Its score for A is the second entry of the blue embedding. Its score for B is one minus that entry. It ignores the other entries. For the circle, the scores are 0.68 and 0.32, so A wins. For the square, the scores are 0.30 and 0.70, so B wins. The same rule gives different answers because different information now reaches the head. This removes the forced tie from Part 8. || 0.8
[[The extra detail helps, but it has no named {c:concept}]] What did we gain, and what did we lose? The blue probability still has a named meaning. The embedding can carry extra detail, including shape in this example. But its coordinates are not supervised as named concepts such as round or square. A CEM can avoid the information loss of the original bottleneck without adding a shape concept. That extra capacity does not make every detail used by the head interpretable. || 0.9
[[Training learns the weights. Testing uses them]] Compare the two stages. The input during training is an image with concept and class labels. At test time it is just a new image. The weights are updated during training and kept fixed at test time. The vectors and probabilities are computed from each image in both cases. || 0.8
[[Concept embedding models in symbols]] In symbols, for concept j out of k: g now outputs a probability and two embeddings, each m numbers long. c hat j is the mixed vector, and f reads all of them. Training still needs images, concept labels and class labels. || 1.2
