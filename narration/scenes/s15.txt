## s15 | Concept embedding models | 0.4 | 0.8
[[One number per {c:concept} is a narrow channel]] First, Concept Embedding Models. One number per concept is a narrow channel, and when the vocabulary is incomplete, accuracy suffers, as we saw in Part 8. || 0.8
[[Intuition: two prototypes per {c:concept}, and a dimmer between them]] Here is the intuition. Give each concept two prototypes, typical points that stand for stripes present and for stripes absent. The probability works like a dimmer switch that slides the concept between the two. || 0.6
[[The prototypes come from the {x:image}, so detail can travel]] Both prototypes are computed from the image itself, so they can differ from one photo to the next. That lets detail beyond yes or no travel to the head. || 0.8
[[Each {c:concept} gets two embeddings: active and inactive]] Now the mechanics. An embedding is a short list of numbers that describes something; here each one has two numbers. From the image, the model computes two embeddings for every concept: one describing it as active, one as inactive. || 0.6
[[The model also predicts the probability {c:p} that the concept is present]] It also predicts the probability, p, that the concept is present. Suppose that, as in Part 4, it misses the stripes on this photo: p is only 0.1. || 0.8
[[The {c:concept embedding} mixes the two, weighted by {c:p}]] The concept's embedding mixes the two: p times the active embedding, plus one minus p times the inactive one. || 0.8
[[With {c:p = 0.1}, the mixed vector sits close to inactive]] With p equal to 0.1, take the first entry: 0.1 times 0.82, plus 0.9 times 0.22, gives 0.28. The second entry works the same way, and the mixed vector sits close to inactive. || 0.8
[[The {f:head} reads all three mixed vectors and wrongly favours {y:horse}]] The head, a linear layer as in Part 4, reads all three concepts, six numbers in all. The stripes vector says inactive, so the head favours horse. || 0.8
[[To {e:intervene}, a person sets {e:p}]] To intervene, a person sets p, not the vector. Setting p to 1 swaps in the active embedding, and zebra now leads. || 0.8
[[Training with random {e:interventions} (RandInt)]] During training, CEM randomly replaces some predicted probabilities with their true values, about one concept in four, so the head learns to respond to corrections. || 0.8
[[Gain: a vector carries more than one number]] The gain is capacity: a vector can carry detail that one number cannot. || 0.6
[[When it helps: the vocabulary is incomplete]] When does that matter? Recall Part 8. With only colour and size as concepts, the blue circle and the blue square got the same concept values, so no head could tell them apart. || 0.6
[[With embeddings, shape can reach the {f:head} without a name]] With embeddings, the vector for blue is computed from each image, so it can differ between the circle and the square. Shape can then reach the head without ever being named. || 0.8
[[Cost: the coordinates of the vector have no names]] The cost is inspectability: the coordinates of that vector have no names, so part of what the head uses cannot be inspected. The detail that separated the circle from the square is exactly the part nobody can read. || 1.0
[[Compared with the plain CBM: each {c:concept} becomes a vector]] Compared with the plain CBM, two of the four things changed. Each concept is now a vector instead of one number, and the head reads those vectors. People still label every concept, and a trained predictor still detects them. || 0.8
[[Concept embedding models in symbols]] In symbols, for concept j out of k: g now outputs a probability and two embeddings, each m numbers long. c hat j is the mixed vector, and f reads all of them. Training still needs images, concept labels and class labels. || 1.2
