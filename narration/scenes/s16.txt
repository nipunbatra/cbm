[[Reuse a {x:pretrained encoder}; build a {c:concept bank}]] Second, post-hoc concept bottleneck models. Instead of training a concept predictor from scratch, they reuse a pretrained encoder that already exists, so nobody has to label every concept on the task training images. We still need a source for the concept bank, which we will build next. || 0.6
[[Intuition: the {x:encoder} already arranges photos along directions]] First, the intuition. A pretrained encoder already arranges photos in its feature space, and some directions in that space follow visual properties, such as how striped an animal is. || 0.6
[[A {c:concept bank} names directions; a score asks how far along]] A concept bank gives names to some of those directions. A concept score then asks one question: how far along the stripes direction is this photo? || 0.8
[[A frozen {x:image encoder} turns each photo into 512 numbers]] Now the details. Here the frozen encoder is CLIP's image encoder: each photograph becomes 512 numbers, which we call e of x. || 0.6
[[Start with examples of one {c:concept}: stripes]] First choose a concept, such as stripes. Collect some images with stripes and some without stripes. These are concept examples: the labels say striped or not striped, rather than zebra or horse. They can come from a separate dataset. Pass both groups through the same frozen image encoder. Only the small concept classifier will be fitted here. || 0.8
[[For intuition, give each image just {x:two features}]] Let us replace 512 numbers with two, so each image becomes a point on a page. The axes are unnamed encoder features, not concepts. [[Fit a boundary, then name the direction across it: {c:CAV}]] Fit a linear classifier to separate the two groups. In two dimensions its boundary is a line. Across that line, towards the striped examples, lies the direction we want. This perpendicular arrow is the concept activation vector, or CAV. It tells us which way increases the striped score. Keep this direction fixed when scoring other images. || 0.8
[[Score a new image along the {c:striped direction}]] Now take a new image. In our toy space its features are 1.5 and 2. The unit CAV points one step to the right and none upwards: its entries are 1, 0. Only the horizontal coordinate contributes: 1.5 times 1 plus 2 times 0 gives 1.5. A second image at minus 0.5, 2 scores minus 0.5. A higher score means further towards the striped side. These are concept scores, not probabilities or animal classes. || 0.8
[[An alternative: CLIP turns the {c:phrase} into a direction]] There is another way to build the concept bank. With CLIP, write a phrase such as black and white stripes and pass it through the pretrained text encoder. It produces a vector in the same space as CLIP image features. Use that vector as the concept direction. This replaces the labelled-example route; it is not another step after fitting a CAV. Both CLIP encoders stay frozen. Our animal example uses this text route. || 0.8
[[A projection: multiply matching entries, then add]] How far along a direction does a photo lie? Multiply the features and the direction entry by entry, then add up the products: this is the dot product. With just two entries, it is the first entry times the first, plus the second times the second. || 0.8
[[Back to {x:512 features}: one score for each {c:phrase}]] Back to the real animal example. Encode each phrase once and save its vector in the concept bank. For a photo, the image encoder produces 512 features. Multiply these features by the matching entries of a phrase vector, then add, just as in our two-dimensional calculation. Repeat for all eight phrases to get eight concept scores. Both vectors have length one, so this dot product is cosine similarity. For our zebra, the raw stripes score is 0.274. These scores are not probabilities. || 0.8
[[Use the same {c:concept prompts} for every {x:image}]] Keep the prompts fixed and change only the image. Here are three held-out photos: a zebra, a horse and a giraffe. Each column uses exactly the same phrase for every photo. Each cell is the raw cosine similarity between that photo and that phrase. This gives one score per image and concept. These scores are not probabilities. || 0.8
[[Each {c:prompt} gets its own training average and spread]] Each prompt has its own usual score and its own spread. To find them, score all 24 training photos with the same prompts. Compute the average and standard deviation separately down each column, across images. Save those training statistics and reuse them for every new photo. The three test photos on the previous slide do not set the scale. || 0.8
[[Standardise each score: subtract the mean, divide by the spread]] Each cell is standardised separately. Take the zebra and stripes cell. Subtract its average over the 24 training photos, then divide by its spread, the standard deviation. For stripes, that gives 1.41. || 0.8
[[The same {x:photos} and {c:prompts}, now on a common scale]] Now apply the same rule to every cell, using its column's saved mean and spread. The photos and prompts have not changed; only the units have changed. The zebra's stripes score becomes 1.41, while the giraffe's long-neck score becomes 1.70. A positive value means above that prompt's training average, zero means at the average, and a negative value means below it. A negative score does not prove that the concept is absent. Within each column, the order of the images stays the same. The head receives these standardised values. || 0.8
[[For this zebra, {c:stripes} scores highest]] These eight numbers are the concept bottleneck. Each belongs to a named phrase. For this zebra photo, black and white stripes scores highest, 1.4 standard deviations above its average on the training photos. || 0.8
[[Train the {f:task head} using concept scores and {y:class labels}]] Now use the task training photos, each with its animal class label. The frozen encoder and concept bank turn each photo into eight scores, using the saved training scale. Feed those scores to a linear head. Compare its prediction with the true class and update only the head weights and constants. The encoder, concept directions and scaling stay fixed. This learns which named concepts help distinguish zebra, horse and giraffe. || 0.8
[[Sparse: only 4 of 24 {f:weights} are non-zero]] The head is also sparse. An L1 penalty adds the size of every weight to the loss, so weights that do not help are pushed to exactly zero. Of 24 possible weights, 8 concepts times 3 classes, only 4 are non-zero: zebra reads stripes, giraffe reads a long neck, and horse reads a flowing mane. || 0.8

[[At test time: one new image, with all weights fixed]] At test time, give the system a new image. The frozen image encoder produces its features. The saved phrase vectors turn them into raw concept scores, and the saved training statistics put the scores on the same scale. The trained head produces class scores; softmax gives probabilities. No concept labels or class label are supplied, and nothing is fitted again. This is the complete prediction path. || 0.8

[[PCBM-h adds an {k:extra path} for evidence the concepts miss]] The concept list may miss useful evidence in the image. PCBM-h adds a residual: an extra linear head that reads the same image features directly. Add its class scores to the concept head's scores, then apply softmax. For our zebra, the score is about 2.03 plus 0.31, giving 2.34 before softmax. During training, keep the concept model fixed and fit only the extra head using class labels. At test time, both paths read the same image. This can improve prediction when the concept list is incomplete. || 0.8
[[An {e:edit} leaves the {k:extra path} unchanged]] Now lower the stripes score, just as before. The concept head sees the edit, but the residual still reads the same image features, so its contribution stays unchanged. After the same edit, the concept model gives zebra probability 0.14; with the residual it is 0.20. The prediction can still change, but some evidence bypasses the concepts. The trade-off is better predictive capacity with less control through named concepts. || 1.0
