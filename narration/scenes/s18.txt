[[LaBo keeps {x:CLIP} itself as the {c:concept} detector]] Label-free models train a linear layer to imitate CLIP. Fourth, LaBo, short for Language in a Bottle, asks a language model for concepts too, but keeps CLIP itself as the concept detector. || 0.8
[[The intuition: let {x:CLIP} read short {c:sentences} about each {y:class}]] The intuition is simple. Describe each class in a few short sentences, let CLIP read them, and use its similarity scores directly as the concepts, written as 100 times CLIP's cosine. For our zebra photo, the stripes sentence scores higher than the mane sentence and the neck sentence. || 0.8
[[Four steps, from sentences to a {f:head} that starts from a guess]] The method has four steps. A language model writes sentences, a selection rule keeps the most useful ones, CLIP scores each image against them, and a linear head, started from a guess, turns the scores into a class. || 0.8
[[Step 1: a language model describes each {y:class} in sentences]] {tags: [calm]} In step one, the language model writes eight short sentences for each class, as JSON. Here they are for zebra. || 0.8
[[A good set of {c:sentences} separates the classes]] Which sentences should we keep? First, each one should separate its class from the others. Take a sentence's average CLIP score on the zebra training photos, and subtract its average on the best other class. Bold black and white stripes has the widest margin of the eight. Stocky horse-like body matches another class better than zebras, so it separates nothing. || 0.8
[[A good set of {c:sentences} does not repeat itself]] Second, the set should not say the same thing three times. Once one stripes sentence is in, a second stripes sentence adds much less that is new. || 0.8
[[Step 2: score each {c:sentence}'s discriminability]] Step two puts numbers on both ideas. Discriminability is the margin from two slides back, rescaled within the class so that the best sentence gets 1 and the worst gets 0. Large striped ears lands at 0.66. || 0.8
[[A greedy rule balances discriminability and coverage]] Coverage gain measures what a sentence adds beyond the sentences already chosen. A greedy rule builds the set one sentence at a time: each round it takes the best mix, 0.7 times discriminability plus 0.3 times coverage gain, and keeps that choice. || 0.8
[[The greedy picks for zebra, in order]] In round one, bold black and white stripes scores 0.7 times 1, plus 0.3 times 0.69, which is 0.91, the best of the eight. Unique facial stripe patterns comes second, then large striped ears. || 0.8
[[Three {c:sentences} per class, nine in all]] For horse, a long flowing mane leads. For giraffe, an extremely long vertical neck. || 0.8
[[Step 3: {x:CLIP} scores the image against each chosen {c:sentence}]] Step three: CLIP scores the image against the nine chosen sentences. Those nine numbers are the bottleneck. Score j compares CLIP's encoding of the image, phi of x, with its encoding of sentence j, psi of s j. || 0.8
[[Step 4: a language prior links each {y:class} to its {c:sentences}]] Step four: a linear layer from sentences to classes, started from a language prior, a starting guess taken from the sentences. Each class gives one third to each of its own three sentences, and nothing to the others. || 0.8
[[With the prior, a {y:class} score is the average of its own {c:sentence} scores]] So each class score is the average of its own three sentence scores. For this zebra, zebra gets 28.1, horse 22.9 and giraffe 23.7. As shares, zebra gets 0.98, and 11 of the 12 test photos are right. || 0.8
[[Why a prior helps when labels are few]] Why start from a prior? A head that starts from zero must learn from the labels which sentence belongs to which class, and that is hard with few labelled photos. The prior already encodes it. A linear probe is a linear layer on CLIP's image features, with no concepts. The LaBo paper reports its clearest advantage over a linear probe with one labelled photo per class. || 0.8
[[Training with the {y:class labels} adjusts the weights]] After that, training with the class labels moves the weights a little, and then all 12 are right. || 0.8
