## s25 | Classification by description | 0.4 | 0.8
[[The last rung: no training data at all]] The last step on the ladder removes training altogether. Menon and Vondrick call it classification by description. We shorten this to CBD in our comparison table. || 0.8
[[Each {y:class} gets a checklist, and {x:CLIP} ticks it softly]] The intuition is a checklist. A language model writes a short checklist for every class. CLIP ticks each item softly, with a score for how well the photo matches it. The class with the best average tick wins. || 1.0
[[A language model describes every {y:class}]] A language model writes a few descriptors for every class: short phrases about what the class looks like. For a herring gull: a white body with black wingtips, a yellow beak and legs, a red spot on its beak. || 0.8
[[{x:CLIP} scores the photo against each descriptor]] CLIP scores the image against each descriptor: that score is the soft tick. The class score is simply the average of its descriptors' scores, and the highest average wins. || 0.8
[[One descriptor score: a {c:cosine} between two vectors]] Where does a score come from? CLIP has two encoders. One, phi, turns the image into a vector; the other, psi, turns the descriptor into a vector in the same space. The score is the cosine similarity of the two: near one when they point the same way, smaller when they do not. || 1.0
[[The {y:class score} averages its descriptor scores]] Then y hat k, the score of class k, averages the descriptor scores over D k, the class's list of descriptors. Nothing is fitted to the target data. || 1.0
[[Every score comes with its reasons]] Every prediction comes with its reasons: the descriptor scores that were averaged. On our gull photograph, the five herring gull descriptors score between 0.32 and 0.34, and their average is about 0.33. || 0.8
[[Slaty-backed gull edges ahead]] But the slaty-backed gull's descriptors average slightly higher, so it edges ahead by a tiny margin. Our photo shows a herring gull, so this prediction is a near miss. || 1.0
[[Attractive when there are no labels at all]] So when is this attractive? When you have no labelled images at all. And a new class needs only a new description: write its checklist, and it can be scored at once, with no retraining. || 1.0
[[A descriptor can be wrong]] But the descriptors themselves can be wrong: the language model says a California gull has a black head, which it does not. || 1.0
[[A {c:match score} is not a fact check]] Look at our herring gull photo: its head is white and its bill is yellow. The California gull checklist says black head and bill, yet CLIP gives that phrase a positive similarity score. That number compares the photo with the whole phrase; it is not a probability that each detail is true. The fixed average still includes it, so a misleading description can affect the class score. Other descriptions may be impossible to verify from a photo, or describe its background. The takeaway: check that the words are correct and visible, then test whether changing that feature changes its score. || 1.2
[[What changed compared with LaBo: the {f:head} is no longer learned]] Compared with LaBo, the head is no longer learned. LaBo trained a linear layer on CLIP's sentence scores; here the class score is a fixed average, so nothing is trained and no labelled image is needed. || 1.0
[[Classification by description in symbols]] In symbols: g is CLIP, c hat d is the similarity to descriptor d, and f is a fixed average over each class's descriptors. The data: none. The cost: nothing guarantees that the descriptors are correct, or grounded. || 1.2
