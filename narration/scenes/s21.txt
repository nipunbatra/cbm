## s21 | Post-hoc or interpretable by design? | 0.4 | 0.8
[[Two routes: explain a model afterwards, or build one you can read]] There are two ways to get an explanation from a model. You can train a black box and explain it afterwards, which is called post-hoc. Or you can build the model so that its reasoning can be read from the start, which is called ante-hoc, or interpretable by design. || 1.0
[[Post-hoc: a method explains a {f:trained model} on one {x:input}]] Start with post-hoc. The model f is already trained. An explanation method takes the model and one input, and returns some evidence about the prediction. || 0.8
[[Saliency: nudge one {x:pixel}, watch one {y:class score}]] How sensitive is the vertical bar score to one pixel? In this teaching example, brighten the centre pixel by 0.1. The score rises by 0.1. We watch the score, even if the predicted class stays the same. || 0.8
[[A saliency map shows where the {y:score} is most sensitive]] Give each pixel the same tiny nudge, one at a time, and compare the size of the score changes. Brighter cells in the saliency map mean a larger change, whether the score rises or falls. Here the middle column matters most for the vertical bar score. || 0.8
[[Occlusion: grey out a {x:patch} and measure how far the {y:score} drops]] A second family changes the input. Grey out one patch, run the model again, and measure how much the score drops. That is the occlusion map we computed for the gull in Part 1. || 0.8
[[Limit 1: an explanation should change when the {f:model} changes]] These methods are useful, and they have limits. The first is a sanity check. If an explanation really describes the model, it should change when the model changes. Adebayo and colleagues tested this by randomising a trained network's weights, layer by layer, until it had learned nothing. || 0.8
[[Some saliency maps barely changed]] Some saliency methods, such as guided backpropagation, still produced almost the same maps for the randomised network. Those maps mostly trace the edges in the image, like an edge detector. So a convincing map is not, on its own, evidence about the model. Plain gradients and Grad-CAM did pass this test. || 1.0
[[Limit 2: a model can learn a {k:shortcut}]] The second limit is about shortcuts. Zech and colleagues trained a pneumonia detector on chest X-rays from two hospital systems. At one hospital about a third of the patients had pneumonia, and at the other about one in a hundred. || 0.8
[[The network could tell which {k:hospital} an X-ray came from]] A network could tell which hospital an X-ray came from almost perfectly, from markers and scanner details rather than from the lungs. Since the hospital predicts pneumonia, the detector could use the hospital as a shortcut. || 0.8
[[An explanation can expose the {k:shortcut}, but the fix is new data]] A heatmap might light up the hospital marker in the corner, and expose the shortcut. But it cannot remove it. On X-rays from a third hospital, the detector did worse. The only fix was new data and retraining. || 1.0
[[Limit 3: a heatmap says {x:where}, not {c:what}]] The third limit is vocabulary. A heatmap says where the score was sensitive. It does not say what the model saw there. On the gull: the yellow bill, the red spot, or the shape of the head? || 0.8
[[Experts speak in {c:findings}, and the model has no knob for them]] Experts reason in named findings: a narrowed joint space, a bone spur, black wingtips. Even when an expert spots the mistake, the model has no named part to correct. There is no setting for wingtip colour that a person could switch from grey to black. || 1.0
[[Ante-hoc: the prediction is computed from quantities a person can read]] Ante-hoc models take the other route. The prediction is computed from quantities a person can read. A sparse linear model on named features is one example. A short decision tree is another. Prototype networks compare parts of an image with stored example parts. || 1.0
[[The case for models that are interpretable by design]] Rudin argued that for high-stakes decisions we should prefer models like these over black boxes explained afterwards. || 1.0
[[A concept bottleneck predicts {c:concepts} first, then the {y:answer}]] {tags: [calm]} A concept bottleneck model is an ante-hoc model for deep networks. First, predict named properties of the photo, its concepts. Then predict the answer from those concepts alone. || 0.8
[[The same path in symbols]] In symbols: x is the input, c hat is the vector of predicted concepts, and y hat is the prediction. A concept predictor, g, maps x to c hat. A task head, f, maps c hat to y hat. || 1.2
